Measured on 2026-09-30 on the development machine (Ryzen 9 7900X, Linux),
branch `mp/d-matrix` on top of the stage D server and client sessions. It holds
the simulator matrix against the [netcode acceptance limits](../MULTIPLAYER.md#netcode-numbers),
the host's processor time and the bandwidth against the
[plan's budget](../multiplayer-plan.md#bandwidth-budget) with 0, 2, 8, 15 and 30
bots on a 15 against 15 mission, and what the CI job covers. Windows and macOS
were not measured; the matrix and the loopback test run on all three in CI.

## The simulator matrix

Code: `crates/tore-session/src/client/matrix_tests.rs`. A host and two bots fly the
scripted fight for each combination of round trip (50, 150 and 300 ms, arrivals
spread by plus or minus 10 percent of the one-way delay) and loss (0, 2 and 5
percent each way, plus 1 percent of packets duplicated), on `tore-world`'s
synthetic fixtures: four friendly aircraft (the two bots and two AI) against
four enemy AI, 1 nautical mile apart at 10,000 feet. Every limit is judged for
each bot on its own, so the worst of the two has to pass; the five-minute form
of all nine cells passes (about 2 minutes 15 seconds in a debug build, four
cells at a time).

| Limit (acceptance table) | How the test measures it |
| --- | --- |
| Own aircraft, same platform, no late input, hit, blast or release in the last second: no correction at all | Every exact state that changed the prediction after the first second of seating, against the host's own record of why each seat's game could not foresee its plane (a repeated input or command at another tick; a change of ownship terms or an event about the plane), within 120 ticks before the exact state's tick. Corrections with no recorded reason must be 0 |
| At least 99 percent of snapshots need no visible correction at up to 2 percent loss, 97 percent at 5 | Corrections of 0.01 ft or 0.01 degrees or more, over the snapshots received |
| Outside the second after a hit, blast or release, 99 percent of corrections are under 1 ft | Corrections with no event reason in the second before |
| Others: 99 percent of frames within 1 ft at no loss, 3 ft at 2 percent, 10 ft at 5 percent | Each other aircraft's drawn position against the host's position at the same moment (the drawn time less its own extra delay), every frame |
| Extrapolated frames under 1 percent at 2 percent loss, under 3 percent at 5 | Entity frames drawn past their newest state, over all entity frames (the 1 percent limit is used at no loss) |
| Inputs the host repeated under 0.5 percent of ticks at up to 2 percent loss, under 2 percent at 5, after the first 5 seconds | The client's count of repeated ticks from the snapshots, less its count 5 seconds after both bots were seated |
| Bandwidth each way per player, against the plan's budget | The host's one-second windows per player, averaged over the run |

Every limit holds in every cell, with a wide margin. What the numbers say:

- **The own aircraft is bit exact.** Over 8,500 to 9,000 snapshots a bot, nothing
  after the first second of seating needed any correction, at any round trip or
  loss. The few exact states that did change the prediction came in the first
  second, while the clock settles (the late inputs of those seconds are what
  the host recorded). The host repeated no input after the first 5 seconds in
  any cell (0 to 0.17 percent of ticks over the whole run, all before).
- **Others** are within the loss's distance in at least 99.76 percent of frames
  (limit 99). The worst errors, 100 to 840 feet, are far aircraft held after a
  lost update, as in the D8a measurement; they are 0.1 to 0.25 percent of
  frames.
- **Extrapolated frames** are 0.4 to 0.5 percent in every cell, loss or not, so
  they are not lost updates. Nearly all of them are far aircraft, the ones
  sent twice a second (475 of about 585 at 150 ms and no loss), drawn past
  their newest state when a band changes; why was not investigated further, and
  it is well inside the limit. The same far aircraft are the worst errors.
- **Bandwidth.** The player's download is 4.0 to 4.8 KB/s on the synthetic
  mission (eight aircraft, little to see), and its upload 3.0 to 4.3 KB/s,
  growing with the round trip because each packet repeats every unacknowledged
  tick: at 300 ms the upload is over the plan's 2 to 4 KB/s estimate. The test
  records both and only fails above twice the budget (agent decision); the real
  mission figures are below.

What the matrix cannot show, and why:

- **The synthetic AI never detects or fires** (a known gap of the fixtures), so
  no aircraft of a bot is ever hit or blasted. Only the bots' own gun fire,
  which changes the plane's ownship terms, exercises the "weapon release" part
  of the rule: 0.5 to 0.9 percent of ticks fall in the second after one. The
  corrections after a hit are measured by the tests of D4 and D8a on the crowd
  fixture, not here.
- **One platform.** Host and bots are the same build in one process, so
  "bit for bit" is shown for one platform only. The cross-platform case (exact
  states every snapshot, corrections too small to show) is for D11's three
  machines.
- **Leaving.** The corrections that follow a bot's Leave (the host gives the
  plane back to the AI while the bot's last controls are neutral) reach 39 ft
  at 300 ms and 5 percent loss. They are not flight and are left out of the
  figures; a game should not draw the plane after the debrief.

## Host cost and bandwidth with real data

Code: `crates/tore-session/tests/host_players.rs`. A 15 against 15 mission (F/A-18Ds
against MiG-29s, average skill, 10 nm apart at 10,000 feet over the Ukraine
theater) from a real 1.02F import, flown for 300 seconds with 0, 2, 8, 15 and
30 `tore_session::bot::Bot` players in one process on the network simulator with
a perfect link, stepped a millisecond at a time. The host's time is its own
thread's processor time around each call into the host (`/proc/thread-self/schedstat`),
so other work on the machine hardly moves it; the bots' work is left out. 15
and fewer bots take friendly planes (`open-planes friendly`), 30 take every
plane (`open-planes all`). Release build.

| Bots | Host cost, first minute | Host cost, minutes 2 to 5 | Whole run | Share of one core | Ticking call: median, 99th percentile, longest | Cost of each human (minutes 2 to 5) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 3.46 ms | 1.27 ms | 1.71 ms | 20.5% | 1.0, 6.0, 8.0 ms | |
| 2 | 3.56 ms | 1.49 ms | 1.90 ms | 22.8% | 2.0, 6.0, 7.0 ms | 0.11 ms a tick |
| 8 | 4.41 ms | 2.29 ms | 2.72 ms | 32.6% | 2.0, 7.0, 9.0 ms | 0.13 ms |
| 15 | 5.56 ms | 2.59 ms | 3.19 ms | 38.3% | 3.0, 8.0, 11.0 ms | 0.09 ms |
| 30 | 6.92 ms | 6.45 ms | 6.54 ms | 78.5% | 6.0, 13.9, 21.8 ms | 0.17 ms |

The first minute is the AI's opening fight, which costs the host the same with
nobody connected (3.4 ms in D7's measurement, 3.46 here); later minutes depend on
how many aircraft are still alive, which is why 15 bots do not cost less than
the straight line from 8 and 30. Each human adds about 0.1 to 0.2 ms to every
tick (not only snapshot ticks: its flight step, inputs, events, readout and
snapshot together), so 30 humans are about 0.65 of a core of host cost on top
of the AI's. A tick is 8.3 ms: with 30 players the ticks that build snapshots
(every fourth, for all 30 players at once) take 14 ms at the 99th percentile
and 22 ms at the longest (before the staggering [below](#after-staggering-the-snapshots-slice-mpd-stagger)), so the host runs those late and catches up (it never
skips time; in the real-time run below that shows as "overloaded" notices when
the machine is also busy).

| Bots | Host to each player (mean of one-second windows, peak) | Host upload in total (mean, peak) | Each player to host | All players to host |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 10.5 KB/s (35.5) | 21 KB/s, 0.17 Mbit/s (71 KB/s) | 2.6 KB/s (2.9) | 5.2 KB/s |
| 8 | 12.3 KB/s (35.2) | 98.5 KB/s, 0.79 Mbit/s (278 KB/s) | 2.6 KB/s (3.0) | 20.8 KB/s |
| 15 | 12.4 KB/s (38.4) | 187 KB/s, 1.49 Mbit/s (524 KB/s) | 2.5 KB/s (3.1) | 38.0 KB/s |
| 30 | 13.8 KB/s (27.5) | 415 KB/s, 3.32 Mbit/s (804 KB/s) | 2.6 KB/s (3.0) | 79.3 KB/s |

Against the plan's budget: each player's download is 10.5 to 13.8 KB/s on average
(budget about 22 KB/s, D6's measurement 13.2), and the peak second 38 KB/s, over
it for a second at a time during missile-heavy moments; the upload is 2.5 to
2.6 KB/s (budget 2 to 4); the host's total upload is 1.49 Mbit/s with 15 players
(budget 2.5 Mbit/s) and 3.32 Mbit/s with 30 (budget about 5 Mbit/s unfiltered,
half with filtering). All figures count the transport's payload bytes, without
the 28 bytes of IP and UDP headers on each packet (about 60 packets a second
from a player, 30 to it).

### After staggering the snapshots (slice mp/d-stagger)

The first rows above were measured with every seat's snapshot built on the same
tick (every fourth). The host now gives each seat its own tick of the four (seat
number modulo 4), so a 30-player server builds at most 8 snapshots on a tick
instead of 30. The 15 and 30 bot runs were repeated with the same command on a
machine at a load average of 2 to 3 (the first runs ran at 2 to 10):

| Bots | | Minute 1 | Minutes 2 to 5 | Whole run | Share of one core | Ticking call: median, 99th percentile, 99.9th, longest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 15 | before | 5.56 ms | 2.59 ms | 3.19 ms | 38.3% | 3.0, 8.0, 9.0, 11.0 ms |
| 15 | staggered | 5.66 ms | 2.76 ms | 3.34 ms | 40.1% | 3.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0 ms |
| 30 | before | 6.92 ms | 6.45 ms | 6.54 ms | 78.5% | 6.0, 13.9, 15.0, 21.8 ms |
| 30 | staggered | 6.80 ms | 6.78 ms | 6.78 ms | 81.4% | 6.0, 10.0, 16.9, 18.5 ms |

The 99th percentile with 30 players falls from 13.9 to 10.0 ms and the longest
tick from 21.8 to 18.5 ms; with 15 players from 8 to 6 ms and from 11 to 8 ms. The
average does not fall (the work is the same, and a snapshot tick now also
rebuilds the sensors' tracked sets for the seats due, 4 times an interval
instead of once; the difference of 0.2 ms with 30 players is within the run to
run spread of the fight, which moves minute 2 by half a millisecond). The
99.9th percentile with 30 players does not fall: those are the AI's heaviest
ticks of the opening fight (the 0-bot run's first minute already has 6 to 8
ms ticks), with a snapshot or two on top, not snapshot spikes. Bandwidth did not
change: 15 players 12.8 KB/s each and 1.54 Mbit/s in total, 30 players 14.0
KB/s each and 3.35 Mbit/s; each player's upload 2.5 to 2.6 KB/s. The matrix's
short form passes unchanged.

### The real programs on loopback

The same mission through the real `tore-server` and `tore-bot` programs (release
builds, one server process, the bots in processes of up to five, UDP on
127.0.0.1, a 300-second run): the script is a few lines of Python that writes the
mission and configuration files (`start now`, `empty-timeout 1`, `after-end
quit`, `status-interval 5`), starts the server, starts the bots a second apart,
waits for them and reads the server's status lines and the bots' own. The server
shares the machine with up to six bot processes and reads wall-clock tick costs,
so this is a cross-check, not the measurement above. Every bot was seated, got its debrief and left cleanly in
every run (2, 8, 15 and 30 bots).

| Bots | Mean tick (status lines, whole run) | Load | Server upload, in total | Server download | Each bot: down, up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 15, friendly open | 3.44 ms (busiest 5 seconds 6.5 ms) | 41% | 186 KB/s (peak 532) | 38 KB/s | 12.1 KB/s, 2.5 KB/s |
| 30, all open | 4.44 ms (8.3) | 53% | 297 KB/s (peak 857) | 78 KB/s | 9.7 KB/s, 2.5 KB/s |

The 15-bot run agrees with the in-process one (186 against 187 KB/s up, 12.1
against 12.4 KB/s a player). The 30-bot run costs less and sends less than the
in-process one (4.4 against 6.5 ms a tick, 297 against 415 KB/s): the bots
joined over about six seconds instead of at once and the fight took its own
course, which is the AI's, not the network's; the mission is not the same
sequence of events twice, and 30 humans' cost is the one to trust from the clean
in-process run. A first batch of the same runs on a machine that other work was
keeping at a load average of 6 to 19 (2, 8, 15 and 30 bots) gave 4.4 to 7.9 ms a
tick and 21 to 145 "overloaded" notices a run, with every bot still leaving
cleanly: the server needs a core to itself, as the
[guide](../DEDICATED-SERVER.md#performance) says.

## What CI covers

The `Network loopback` workflow (`.github/workflows/network.yml`) runs
`crates/tore-session/tests/loopback.rs` on Ubuntu 22.04, Windows 2022 and
macOS 14: the host in the test process on a real socket, with the real clock and
system entropy, and two real `tore-bot` processes, each loading a synthetic
import the test writes (the fixtures with the import's four markers), taking a
plane, flying 20 seconds of the scripted fight and leaving. It fails on any
refusal, fault, content mismatch, seat refusal, a player that did not leave by
its own Leave (a drop), any bot that does not exit cleanly, or a host that still
lists a player. The short matrix is part of `cargo test --workspace` on every
platform. The Linux run passed locally; Windows and macOS were not run here.
